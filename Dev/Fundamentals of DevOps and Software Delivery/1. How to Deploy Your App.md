> *I wrote an app. Now what?*

# Platform as a Service
In a matter of minutes, a good PaaS can take care of so many software delivery concerns for you.
The greatest weakness is that if something doesn't work, **debugging or fixing it can be hard.**
# Infrastructure as a Code
The greatest strength and weakness of IaaS: you have more control and visibility, so you have fewer limits, can customize things more, and meet wider set of requirements; but for those same reasons, it's much more work than using a PaaS.
## Virtual private server (VPS)
The main reason you'd go with one of these providers is that you just want a **replacement for having to rack your own servers**, and you prefer to have someone else do it for you and give you access.
## Content delivery networks (CDN)
The main reason you'd go with one of these providers is that your user base is **geographically distributed**, and you need a fast and reliable way to serve them content with low latency.
## Cloud providers
General-purpose cloud solutions that offer everything.

> In general, the VPS and CDN providers are specialist. So, if you need ***only*** those specific items, you're better off going with a specialist.

> Using the cloud should be your default choice for most new deployments.
# When to go with:
## On prem
- You already have an on/prem presence
- You have usage patterns that are better fit for on prem
	- Steady, predictable usage that doesn't benefit from elasticity.
	- Usage that requires lots of bandwidth (Expensive in Cloud).
- You have compliance requirements
	- Standards, regulations, laws.
- Need more control over pricing.
	- A vendor will abruptly raise prices.
## Hybrid
- Partial cloud migration
- Right tool for the job
	- Use cloud when you need elasticity
	- Use on prem when you have steady traffic patterns
## Cloud
- Elasticity and pay as you go
- Speed
- Maintenance and expertise
- Managed services
- Security
- Global reach

> Go with PaaS whenever you can; ho with IaaS when you have to.

### PaaS
- Side projects
- Startups and small companies
- New and experimental projects
### IaaS
- Load (lot of traffic)
- Company size
- Availability
- Security and compliance

> You should spend as little time on software delivery as you possibly can while still meeting your company's requirements.

# Adopting DevOps Practices

> Adopt the architecture and software delivery processes that are appropriate for you stage of company.

Most large software projects fail. Only 1 out of 10 large projects are completed on time and on budget.
## Incrementalism
Do it incrementally. The key to incrementalism is splitting job in such a way that every step brings its own value.
## False incrementalism
You've split the work into smaller steps, but you get value only when all the steps are completed. You get the worst possible outcome: you've invested a bunch of time and money but got nothing in return.

> Adopt DevOps incrementally, as a series of small steps, where each step is valuable by itself.

For example, instead of trying to do a "big bang" migration to the cloud, try to identify one team that is struggling and then work to migrate just that team.

# Summary

> Always aim for the minimum effective dose of DevOps.

You want to use **the simplest and most minimal solution** that gives you the benefits you're looking for, and no more.
